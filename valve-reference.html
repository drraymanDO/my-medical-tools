Valvular Heart Disease — Echo Reference

Grading criteria for Aortic Stenosis, Aortic Regurgitation, Mitral Stenosis, and Mitral Regurgitation (ASE/EACVI-based)

Primary Grading Parameters Continuous-wave Doppler

Parameter Normal Mild Moderate Severe
Aortic Jet Velocity (Vmax) < 2.0 m/s 2.0–2.9 m/s 3.0–3.9 m/s ≥ 4.0 m/s
Mean Gradient — < 20 mmHg 20–39 mmHg ≥ 40 mmHg
Aortic Valve Area (AVA) 2.6–3.5 cm² > 1.5 cm² 1.0–1.5 cm² < 1.0 cm²
Indexed AVA (AVAi) — > 0.85 cm²/m² 0.60–0.85 cm²/m² < 0.6 cm²/m²
Dimensionless Index (DVI) ≥ 0.50 > 0.50 0.25–0.50 < 0.25
AVA calculation (continuity equation): AVA = (LVOT area × LVOT VTI) / AV VTI.
DVI (dimensionless index) = LVOT VTI / AV VTI — useful because it's independent of LVOT diameter measurement error, which is the biggest source of error in the continuity equation.

Special Situations & Pitfalls

ScenarioIssueApproach
Low-flow, low-gradient AS (reduced EF) AVA <1.0 cm² but mean gradient <40 mmHg due to low stroke volume Dobutamine stress echo to assess contractile reserve & true severity
Low-flow, low-gradient AS (preserved EF) "Paradoxical" low-flow state — small, hypertrophied LV with low stroke volume index despite normal EF Stroke volume index <35 mL/m² confirms low-flow state; consider CT calcium scoring
High-flow states Anemia, AV fistula, hyperthyroidism can elevate gradients despite non-severe AS Correlate with AVA & clinical context, not gradient alone
Aortic valve calcium score (CT): Used to confirm severity when Doppler/echo parameters are discordant. General thresholds: Men — severe likely ≥2000 AU (unlikely <1600 AU); Women — severe likely ≥1200 AU (unlikely <800 AU).

Supportive / Qualitative Findings

  • Valve morphology: degree of leaflet calcification/restriction on 2D imaging (heavy calcification supports severe AS)
  • LV hypertrophy: concentric LVH is a compensatory response to chronic pressure overload
  • LV function: reduced EF in advanced disease reflects afterload mismatch/decompensation
  • Acceleration time / ejection time ratio: used in some algorithms, particularly intraoperative TEE, when spectral Doppler alignment is imperfect

Quantitative parameters (regurgitant volume, RF, EROA) are typically derived via the PISA method in AR, though it's technically more challenging than in mitral regurgitation due to the aortic valve's geometry and is used less routinely in everyday practice compared to the semi-quantitative measures above.

Structural / Consequential Findings

Mild
Normal LV size
Moderate
Normal or mildly dilated LV
Severe
LV dilation (chronic)
Chronic severe AR leads to LV eccentric hypertrophy/dilation as the ventricle compensates for the combined volume overload. LV end-systolic diameter (LVESD) and LVEF trending are central to timing surgical referral — intervention is generally considered once LVESD > 50 mm (or indexed >25 mm/m²) or LVEF ≤ 55%, even in asymptomatic patients, per current guidelines.

Acute vs. Chronic AR — Key Distinction

FeatureAcute Severe ARChronic Severe AR
LV size Normal (no time to remodel) Dilated (eccentric hypertrophy)
Hemodynamic tolerance Poor — non-compliant LV, high filling pressures Often well-tolerated for years
Mitral valve Premature MV closure on M-mode — key sign Normal MV closure timing
Typical causes Aortic dissection, endocarditis, trauma Bicuspid valve, annular dilation, degenerative disease
Clinical picture Acute pulmonary edema / cardiogenic shock Gradual exertional dyspnea over years
Acute severe AR is a surgical emergency — the non-dilated, non-compliant LV cannot accommodate the sudden regurgitant volume, leading to rapid decompensation. Premature diastolic mitral valve closure on echo is a critical clue not to miss.

Primary Grading Parameters 2D & Doppler

Parameter Normal Mild Moderate Severe
Mitral Valve Area (MVA) 4–6 cm² > 1.5 cm² 1.0–1.5 cm² < 1.0 cm²
Mean Gradient — < 5 mmHg 5–10 mmHg > 10 mmHg
Pressure Half-Time (PHT) — < 150 ms 150–219 ms ≥ 220 ms
Pulmonary Artery Systolic Pressure < 30 mmHg < 30 mmHg 30–50 mmHg > 50 mmHg
MVA by Pressure Half-Time: MVA = 220 / PHT. Quick and widely used, but affected by LV/LA compliance, AR, and immediately post-valvuloplasty (unreliable in the first 24-48h post-intervention).
MVA by Planimetry (2D short-axis): most direct anatomic measurement — considered the reference method when image quality allows tracing the true leaflet tips at the smallest orifice.
MVA by Continuity Equation: MVA = (LVOT area × LVOT VTI) / MV VTI — useful when PHT is unreliable (e.g., concomitant AR, arrhythmia) but requires no more than mild AR/MR to be valid.

Special Situations & Pitfalls

ScenarioIssueApproach
Atrial fibrillation Beat-to-beat variability in filling time and gradient Average measurements over 5-10 beats
Concomitant AR PHT shortened/unreliable due to faster LV pressure rise from combined MS+AR filling Favor planimetry or continuity equation over PHT
Tachycardia / short diastolic filling Gradients can be overestimated due to incomplete diastasis Correlate with MVA rather than gradient alone; consider rate control before final assessment
Post-valvuloplasty (early) PHT unreliable in first 24-48h due to acute compliance changes Rely on planimetry/continuity in the immediate post-procedure period

Morphology Scoring — Suitability for Valvuloplasty

Wilkins Score (0-4 each, total 0-16) grades four features: leaflet mobility, leaflet thickening, subvalvular thickening, and calcification. A total score ≤ 8 generally favors a good outcome with percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV), while higher scores favor surgical intervention.
  • Leaflet mobility: restriction of leaflet tips vs. entire leaflet/subvalvular apparatus
  • Leaflet thickening: near-normal (4-5mm) vs. markedly thickened (>8-10mm)
  • Subvalvular thickening: minimal chordal thickening vs. extensive fusion/shortening
  • Calcification: single area of echo brightness vs. extensive calcification through most of leaflet tissue
Commissural fusion pattern, degree of MR, and presence of LA thrombus (on TEE) are also essential to assess before PBMV — significant MR or LA appendage thrombus are contraindications regardless of Wilkins score.

Associated / Downstream Findings

  • Left atrial enlargement: chronic pressure/volume overload from obstructed LA emptying — degree often correlates with disease chronicity and AFib risk
  • Pulmonary hypertension: develops from chronically elevated LA pressure transmitted backward — PASP is both a severity marker and a trigger for intervention consideration
  • RV dysfunction: can develop secondary to long-standing pulmonary hypertension (ties back to the RV strain concepts — TAPSE, RV size, in this context reflect downstream consequences of MS rather than a primary RV problem)
  • LA thrombus: stagnant flow in a dilated, poorly-contracting LA (especially with concurrent AFib) raises thromboembolic risk — critical to exclude via TEE prior to valvuloplasty or cardioversion

Quantitative Parameters PISA-based

Parameter Mild Moderate Severe
Regurgitant Volume < 30 mL/beat 30–59 mL/beat ≥ 60 mL/beat
Regurgitant Fraction < 30% 30–49% ≥ 50%
Effective Regurgitant Orifice Area (EROA) < 0.20 cm² 0.20–0.39 cm² ≥ 0.40 cm²
PISA (Proximal Isovelocity Surface Area) method is the most widely used quantitative approach for MR — it models flow convergence proximal to the regurgitant orifice to derive EROA and regurgitant volume. Note the EROA severe threshold differs from AR (≥0.40 cm² vs. ≥0.30 cm²) — a common mix-up.
Primary vs. secondary MR EROA thresholds differ in the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines — secondary (functional) MR is considered severe at a lower EROA (≥0.40 cm²) and regurgitant volume (≥60 mL) than the classic primary MR thresholds used historically, reflecting that even modest functional MR carries prognostic weight in HF populations.

Primary vs. Secondary (Functional) MR

FeaturePrimary (Organic) MRSecondary (Functional) MR
Valve leaflets Structurally abnormal (prolapse, flail, rheumatic, endocarditis) Structurally normal
Mechanism Intrinsic leaflet/chordal pathology LV dilation/dysfunction or annular dilation pulls leaflets apart (tethering)
Typical etiology Myxomatous degeneration, rheumatic disease, endocarditis, chordal rupture Ischemic or non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, LA dilation (atrial functional MR)
Management implication Often surgical repair/replacement once severe, even if asymptomatic with LV changes Treat underlying LV disease first (GDMT); intervention (repair or transcatheter edge-to-edge repair) reserved for select persistent severe cases
This distinction matters enormously for management — secondary MR severity is disease-dependent and can improve with optimized heart failure therapy, whereas primary MR from a structural lesion generally does not improve without mechanical intervention.

Structural / Consequential Findings

  • LV size: chronic severe MR causes eccentric LV dilation as the ventricle accommodates combined forward + regurgitant volume
  • LV end-systolic diameter (LVESD) & EF: central to surgical timing in primary MR — intervention generally considered once LVESD ≥ 40 mm or LVEF ≤ 60%, even if asymptomatic, per current guidelines
  • LA size: chronic volume overload leads to LA enlargement; a marker of chronicity and AFib risk
  • Pulmonary artery pressure: elevated PASP is both a severity marker and, in secondary MR, a factor supporting intervention in persistently symptomatic patients on GDMT

Acute vs. Chronic MR — Key Distinction

FeatureAcute Severe MRChronic Severe MR
LA/LV size Normal/near-normal (no time to remodel) Dilated LA and LV
Color jet size Can appear deceptively small/eccentric despite severe regurgitation — because of high LA pressure equalizing quickly Often more holosystolic, better reflects volume by color proportions
Hemodynamic tolerance Poor — non-compliant LA causes rapid pulmonary edema Often well-tolerated for years due to LA/LV compensation
Typical causes Papillary muscle rupture (post-MI), chordal rupture, endocarditis Myxomatous degeneration, ischemic/non-ischemic cardiomyopathy
Pitfall: in acute severe MR, a deceptively unimpressive-looking color jet can lead to underestimation of severity. Always correlate with clinical presentation (flash pulmonary edema, new murmur post-MI) and consider TEE if suspicion is high despite modest-appearing TTE color Doppler.
Reference summary for educational purposes — always correlate with clinical context, guidelines (ASE/EACVI), and integrate multiple parameters rather than relying on any single value.
